A Remote Eligibility Email That Gets a Clear Answer

Many remote job listings hide the real constraints: which countries are eligible, how many overlap hours are required. A vague email gets a vague answer. This free worked example shows how to fill in a one-paragraph message that makes it easy for a hiring team to reply with a clear yes or no.

Before: the template with brackets

Subject: Location eligibility for [role]

"Hello [name/team], your [role] listing mentions [exact location or working-hours statement]. I am based in [country] and can work [specific overlap hours and time zone]. Is this location and schedule eligible for the role? Thank you, [your name]."

After: filled in (fictional applicant)

Subject: Location eligibility for Senior Backend Developer

"Hello Maria, your Senior Backend Developer listing mentions 'remote, with 4 hours of overlap with CET.' I am based in Brazil and can work 08:00-13:00 CET (03:00-08:00 Brasilia time), which is 5 hours of overlap. Is this location and schedule eligible for the role? Thank you, Ana."

Why the filled-in version works

  • It quotes the listing's exact words ("remote, with 4 hours of overlap with CET"), so the reader knows precisely which constraint is being asked about.
  • It converts your situation into their timezone, "08:00-13:00 CET" means the reader does no mental math.
  • It over-delivers on the requirement, 5 hours of offered overlap against 4 required is shown, not claimed in adjectives.
  • It asks one closing question, which invites a one-line answer.

The rule behind it

Replace every bracket with something specific and true, keep it to one screen, and make the reader's reply as easy as possible. A second example: the short follow-up variant

The example above shows a full first message. Most applications also need a shorter companion: the follow-up you send when a listing has been quiet for a week. It uses the same structure at half the length, and it is the message developers most often get wrong by either over-explaining or skipping entirely.

Subject: Following up - Senior Backend Engineer application

Hello [name],

I applied for the Senior Backend Engineer role on [date]. I am following up once to confirm my application reached you, and to add one line I left out: the listing asks for experience with event-driven systems, and my most recent project processed roughly 40,000 events per day using Kafka, which I would be glad to walk through.

If the role has been filled or the process has moved on, no reply is needed - I will close it out on my side.

Thank you for your time,
[your name]

Why this variant works

Three deliberate choices make this follow-up effective. First, it states the application date immediately, so the reader does not have to search their inbox. Second, the added line is one requirement-to-proof sentence, the same evidence-mapping discipline as the first message, not a restatement of the whole application. Third, it offers an exit: telling the reader no reply is needed removes the awkwardness that stops busy hiring managers from answering short emails at all, and a courteous close-out still leaves the door open for the next role.

Adapting the two templates

Use the full template for a first application and the short variant exactly once per application, about a week later. Replace every bracketed field, then read the message aloud once: any sentence you stumble over is a sentence the reader will stumble over too. Keep both versions per application in one place, so when a reply arrives you know exactly what you already said. Reading the reply: three answers and what each one means

Once the message goes out, three kinds of reply come back, and each has exactly one right response:

  1. A clear yes. "You are eligible, please apply" or a link to the next step. Act the same day while the context is fresh in their inbox, and note the name of whoever replied - it becomes your reference in every later message for that application.
  2. A clear no. "This role is EU-only" or similar. Close the row immediately and do not ask follow-up questions about why; the constraint is theirs, not an invitation to negotiate. A closed "not eligible" row is useful data: it tells you which listing sources produce roles you can actually take.
  3. The non-answer. "We are still reviewing" or silence after another week. Treat silence as a soft no, re-ask once at most using the short follow-up variant above, then close out. Teams that wanted you rarely lose an eligible candidate over timezone arithmetic - the message above made your case in five lines.

Writing down which of the three you received, on the same day it arrives, is what turns scattered emails into a pipeline you can actually manage. This is the same reason the kit's tracker has a status column per row: the memory of "I think they seemed interested" is not a status, "clear yes, 2026-10-14, Maria replied" is.

Subject-line discipline

Both subjects in the examples above follow one rule: role, plus purpose, nothing else. "Location eligibility for Senior Backend Developer" tells a hiring manager what the email is about and which role it concerns before they open it. The subject is doing real work: hiring teams triage dozens of applications, and a message that must be opened to be understood is a message that gets read last.

Patterns to avoid: bare greetings ("Hi" or "Quick question"), which read as cold outreach; ALL CAPS or the word "urgent", which reads as pressure from a stranger; and reusing an old thread for a new role, which buries the actual question under a stale history. If you cannot fit the role and the purpose into one line, shorten the role title, not the purpose.
